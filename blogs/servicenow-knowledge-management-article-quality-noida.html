Creating Useful Knowledge Articles in ServiceNow: A Practical Guide

A support team may solve the same issue many times because the solution is difficult to find, incomplete, or written only in an individual technician's notes. Knowledge management helps turn repeatable solutions into guidance that employees and support teams can use. This guide walks through a practical approach to creating and maintaining useful knowledge content in ServiceNow.

ServiceNow learning guide · 7 min read

Start with a problem people actually need to solve

Imagine employees regularly asking how to reconnect to a corporate Wi-Fi network after changing their password. A useful knowledge article should help the reader recognise the situation, follow safe steps, and know when to contact support. It should not simply repeat technical notes from the person who resolved the last ticket.

Before drafting, review the recurring questions or incidents behind the topic. Confirm the intended audience, the approved procedure, and whether the instructions are still accurate. Do not publish unverified workarounds or include passwords, personal data, or other sensitive information.

1. Give the article a clear purpose and title

A title should describe the task or question in the language the reader is likely to use. “Reconnect to the corporate Wi-Fi after a password change” is more useful than a vague title such as “Network issue”. Keep one main problem or procedure in each article so search results remain specific.

Write a short opening that explains who the article is for and when it applies. If there are prerequisites, such as being connected to the company network or having an approved device, state them before the steps begin.

2. Organise the instructions for scanning

Use a simple structure: purpose, symptoms or applicability, prerequisites, numbered steps, expected result, and escalation guidance. Keep each step focused on one action. If a screen label or menu name matters, use the exact wording shown in the relevant environment and review it after interface changes.

For example, separate “Check that the device is using the current password” from “Remove and reconnect to the saved network”. Add screenshots only when they clarify a step, and ensure they do not expose real employee names, email addresses, device identifiers, or internal secrets.

3. Make the article easy to find

Think about the phrases a reader might search for: “Wi-Fi password changed”, “cannot connect after password reset”, or “reconnect laptop to Wi-Fi”. Use natural wording in the title and body, and choose the categories or metadata your knowledge process supports. Avoid stuffing a list of unrelated search phrases into the article.

Where appropriate, connect the knowledge content to the relevant service, category, or support process. A well-written article is less useful if it is filed somewhere readers and agents will not look.

4. Review accuracy before publishing

Ask a subject-matter expert or the team that owns the procedure to validate the instructions. A reviewer should check whether the steps work, whether the language is understandable to the target audience, and whether the article reveals information that should not be public to that audience.

In a ServiceNow learning instance, explore how knowledge records move through draft, review, publication, and retirement according to the configuration. The exact workflow, roles, and approval requirements can vary by organisation, so use your instance's rules rather than assuming every environment is identical.

5. Test the article as a reader

Give the draft to someone who did not write it. Ask them to follow the steps without extra explanation. Note where they hesitate, which terms are unfamiliar, and whether the expected result is obvious. If the reader needs to ask the author what a step means, the article probably needs a clearer instruction.

Also check the experience from the intended user perspective. Confirm that the audience can access the article and that any referenced forms, links, or support channels are available to them.

6. Keep knowledge content current

Procedures change when applications, policies, devices, or support ownership changes. Assign an owner and a review date where your knowledge process allows it. When a related incident shows that an instruction is confusing or outdated, use that feedback to improve the article.

Retire content that is no longer valid rather than leaving old instructions alongside the current procedure. If two articles cover the same task, consider whether one should be updated or the content combined to reduce confusion.

7. Use feedback to improve the knowledge base

Look beyond the number of published articles. Useful signals may include whether readers found an answer, whether support agents reuse the article, whether the same issue continues to generate tickets, and what feedback readers leave. These measures should guide review, not become targets that encourage publishing low-quality material.

A short, accurate article that resolves a frequent question can be more valuable than a long collection of loosely related tips.

A simple practice exercise for learners

Choose a harmless, familiar support question in a practice environment. Draft a knowledge article with a descriptive title, audience, prerequisites, numbered steps, expected outcome, and escalation route. Ask another learner to test it, revise unclear wording, and document what changed after review.

Key takeaways

  • Base knowledge articles on recurring, verified support needs.
  • Use task-focused titles and a predictable structure.
  • Write steps for the intended reader, not only for technical specialists.
  • Review access, accuracy, and sensitive information before publishing.
  • Assign ownership and update or retire articles as processes change.

Knowledge management works best as an ongoing practice, not a one-time writing task. Learning to create, review, and maintain clear articles gives ServiceNow learners a practical view of how support teams share knowledge and improve service experiences.
